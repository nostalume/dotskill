# Representation Selection

Select a representation by constructing a hypothesis about how the intended
audience can recover the required meaning and act. Do not begin from a chart
catalog, available template or favored library. The same information can require
different representations when the question, audience, setting or intended
response changes.

## Admit the visual problem

Use authoritative source and current project evidence to establish:

- the question or experience the visual must support;
- who will use it, under what attention, knowledge and viewing conditions;
- the intended comparison, decision, action or feeling;
- the grain, scope, units, time and uncertainty of the information;
- facts, relationships and qualifications that must survive;
- required editability, carrier and non-visual access; and
- the misunderstanding or unusable outcome that would constitute failure.

Ask only for a material missing decision. Otherwise make the smallest reversible
hypothesis that keeps uncertainty visible. Do not let an input filename, existing
template or implementation language decide the information structure.

## Classify meaning before form

A visual may combine several structures. Preserve each structure's laws rather
than flattening everything into one universal model.

| Structure | Meaning to preserve | Questions that shape the hypothesis |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Quantitative | values, units, grain, aggregation, scale, baselines, comparisons and uncertainty | Is the task magnitude, difference, distribution, trend, part-to-whole, rank or lookup? Which comparisons must be perceptually direct? |
| Relational | entities, edge meaning, direction, grouping, hierarchy and known/unknown links | Is the claim connection, dependency, flow, containment, sequence or causality? Which relationships must be traced? |
| Spatial | position, coordinate/projection meaning, resolution, scale, coverage and spatial uncertainty | Is geography itself explanatory, or only a grouping key? What distortion and missing coverage matter? |
| Sequential | order, dependency, branching, concurrency and completion state | Must the audience follow steps, compare states or understand alternatives? Does order imply causality or merely chronology? |
| Temporal | state over selected time, rate, duration, continuity and synchronization | Does change itself carry meaning that static juxtaposition cannot? What must remain readable at each moment? |
| Interactive | initial state, possible actions, state transitions, empty/error states and fallback meaning | Does interaction enable a necessary question or merely hide information? Can the core result survive reduced capability? |
| Explanatory | accepted content, evidence, annotation, narrative hierarchy and source qualification | Which observation is primary, what context prevents misreading, and what should attention encounter next? |

## Construct a representation hypothesis

1. Name the elements the audience must distinguish and the comparisons or paths
   they must perform.
2. Assign the most perceptually reliable available channels to the most important
   distinctions. Position and aligned length often support precise comparison;
   area, angle, color and motion can support other goals but need context and
   should not imply unsupported precision.
3. Choose grouping, order, annotation and views so the intended path is easy while
   alternative readings remain honest. Reduce competing signals before adding
   decoration.
4. Choose static, temporal or interactive behavior only when it preserves or
   reveals meaning better than a simpler carrier under the actual use conditions.
5. Name material loss: what becomes harder to see, what is aggregated, omitted or
   schematic, and what an attractive alternative would distort. Record rejected
   alternatives only when the tradeoff matters to later work.

This hypothesis is revisable. A rendered or exercised result may reveal that the
chosen density, hierarchy, carrier or even information structure is wrong. Return
to the earliest contradicted decision instead of treating every defect as styling.

## Preserve structure-specific invariants

### Quantitative

Keep source grain, units, aggregation, denominator, time basis, missingness and
uncertainty visible to the degree needed for the claim. Length comparisons need a
meaningful common baseline; centered deviations and indexed views may use another
reference when it is explicit. A cropped scale must not manufacture visual
magnitude. Keep area and volume proportional to the represented quantity when
they encode magnitude. Distinguish observed values, estimates, forecasts and
targets. A table may be superior when exact lookup matters more than pattern;
combine views only when each has a real use.

### Relational and sequential

Define what a node, edge, arrow, enclosure, rank and repeated element mean. Layout
proximity or visual direction must not invent dependency, hierarchy or causality.
Make important paths traceable; manage crossings, labels and repeated structures
according to the reading task. Show omitted, uncertain or cyclic relationships
when their absence would mislead. A process diagram distinguishes required order,
optional branches, concurrency and terminal states.

### Spatial

Use a map only when location or spatial relationship helps answer the question.
State or preserve the coordinate system, projection, geographic unit, resolution,
coverage and uncertainty that affect interpretation. Normalize by the relevant
population or area when raw totals would answer a different question. Do not let
symbol area, map area or color steps imply unsupported comparison.

### Temporal and interactive

Motion must reveal order, change, continuity, spatial relation or attention—not
merely decorate. Interaction must enable a necessary exploration or action and
retain understandable initial, changed, empty, loading, error and reduced-
capability states where applicable. A static alternative or summary remains valid
when the intended meaning does not require the runtime. Use the dedicated
temporal/interaction route only after that value is established.

### Explanatory composition

Preserve accepted wording, imagery, attribution and evidence. Establish one
perceivable primary path, then support it with context, annotation and source
qualification. Composition can create emphasis but must not promote speculation
over evidence or imply a relationship absent from the source.

## Vary deliberately

Hierarchy, density, grouping, whitespace, typography, color, texture, annotation,
imagery, motion and interaction are design axes, not fixed rules. Select them from
audience, medium, brand, accessibility, content and emotional register. A project
system is authoritative when present. An unconventional choice is valid when it
serves the visual contract and passes applicable checks; novelty alone is not
quality.

Compare alternatives by the audience operations they enable, the meaning they
lose, their cognitive and implementation cost, their editability and the evidence
available to validate them. The chosen representation is ready for implementation
when its invariant mapping, contextual rationale, material loss and validation
obligations are clear enough to falsify.
